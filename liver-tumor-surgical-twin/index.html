FEM Paradigms for Heterogeneity Modeling in
Multi-Material Tissue-Tumor Surgical Twins

1School of Mechanical Engineering, Purdue University  ·  2Indiana University School of Medicine
3School of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Purdue University  ·  4Department of Computer Science, Purdue University

Abstract

Building a digital twin of a patient organ for real-time surgical rehearsal requires considering several parameters: how to represent a stiff inclusion, such as a tumor, inside softer surrounding tissue; boundary conditions, and constraints. The choice of formulation determines whether the model survives gravity, whether the interface between the two materials stays intact under instrument contact, and whether the simulation still runs fast enough to be interactive.

We compare three finite element formulations on a single CT-derived ex vivo liver scene. The first treats the tumor and the liver as separate volumetric meshes held by fixed constraints. The second couples two separate meshes with attachment constraints. The third uses one continuous volumetric mesh partitioned by convex-hull regions of interest, with distinct elastic parameters assigned per subregion. Each formulation is settled under gravity and then palpated by a da Vinci Research Kit instrument inside the SOFA framework.

We find that both multi-body formulations lose interface integrity, with the tumor separating from the liver cavity under full gravity, while the heterogeneous single-mesh formulation preserves continuity by construction and resolves a positive, monotonic apparent stiffness contrast under palpation while remaining above interactive frame rates.

Three formulations, one scene

Every formulation is run on the same liver geometry, the same gravity levels, and the same palpation targets, so the only variable is how heterogeneity is represented.

Formulation A

Uncoupled multi-body

Tumor and liver are separate volumetric meshes with uniform properties each, held in place by fixed constraints. Simple to build, but nothing physically binds the two surfaces together.

Formulation B

Constraint-coupled multi-body

The same two meshes, now joined by attachment constraints across the shared interface. The constraint is an added artefact rather than a property of the material itself.

Formulation C

Heterogeneous single-mesh

One continuous mesh, partitioned by convex-hull regions of interest, each region given its own Young's modulus and Poisson ratio. Interface nodes are shared, so continuity holds by construction.

Interface integrity under gravity

Before any instrument touches the organ, the model must simply hold together under its own weight. We settle each formulation at one tenth of gravity and at full gravity, then measure the signed distance across every interface node pair. A pair separated by more than one millimetre counts as a failure.

Formulation Failed pairs at 0.1 g Failed pairs at 1.0 g Interface preserved
A   Uncoupled multi-body 0.5 percent 100 percent No
B   Constraint-coupled multi-body 57 percent 73 percent No
C   Heterogeneous single-mesh 0 percent 0 percent Yes

Under full gravity the constraint-coupled model shows interpenetration and separation occurring at the same time in different regions of the interface, which is the signature of a constraint fighting the underlying elasticity rather than expressing it.

Palpation response and stiffness discrimination

A Cadiere forceps instrument indents the liver directly above the embedded tumor, and the response is recorded at four stages of the approach. Each row below shows the same four stages under a different visualisation, so the surface behaviour, the internal mesh behaviour and the stress field can be read against one another at matched depth.

Quantitatively, five fixed-depth palpation points are indented at matched positions across four material configurations. Local contact stiffness is the slope of contact force against indentation depth, computed by sliding-window regression. Apparent contrast is that slope over the tumor divided by the slope on a same-mesh homogeneous control, in which the tumor region of interest is assigned the same modulus as the surrounding parenchyma (Eliver = Etumor = 5.5 kPa), evaluated at matched indentation depth.

Tumor region modulus Contrast range across 5 points Mean contrast Frame rate
5.5 kPa (homogeneous control) 1.00 by definition 1.00 40 to 50 fps
55 kPa 1.164 to 1.377 1.237 35 fps
75 kPa 1.195 to 1.499 1.300 30 to 32 fps

Apparent contrast is well below the underlying ten times material ratio. This is the physically expected outcome for an inclusion embedded within one centimetre of the surface, and it is why contrast is reported as a curve against depth rather than as a single number.

Video results

Palpation under two solver configurations

The two configurations sit at opposite ends of the same trade off. The direct solver produces a physically meaningful compliance and therefore trustworthy contact forces, but it falls below interactive rates on this mesh. The iterative solver holds interactive rates comfortably, but its constraint correction cannot express the stiffness of the material, so the extracted contact forces are unreliable as a basis for stiffness discrimination. Resolving this tradeoff is the motivation for the compliant force sensor embodiment at the instrument tip, for a future study.

Patient-specific scene generation

Tumor cavity construction and mesh generation are automated through Autodesk Fusion, GMSH and a Python interface, producing matched finite element tetrahedral volumetric meshes, collision and visual meshes for the simulator. A single configuration is generated in between 0.005 and 0.65 seconds, which makes it practical to sweep tumor size, depth and position for synthetic data generation.
